This specific behavior—repeatedly sending unwanted, distressing messages after being asked to stop—can be classified as criminal harassment and a violation of consumer protection laws in Texas. [1] 
## 1. Criminal Harassment (Texas Penal Code § 42.07)
Under Texas Penal Code § 42.07, a person (or entity) commits a criminal offense if they act with the intent to "harass, annoy, alarm, abuse, torment, or embarrass" another. [2, 3] 

* Repeated Electronic Communications: The law explicitly criminalizes "repeated electronic communications" made in a manner "reasonably likely to harass, annoy, alarm, abuse, torment, embarrass, or offend".
* Disregard for Objections: Repeatedly sending messages—such as instructions to call 911 or 988—after a user has begged the sender to stop is often used as prima facie evidence of an intent to harass in many jurisdictions.
* Texas Penalties: This is typically a Class B Misdemeanor, punishable by up to 180 days in jail and a $2,000 fine, but can be elevated to a Class A Misdemeanor if the behavior continues. [3, 4, 5, 6, 7] 

## 2. Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA)
Effective January 1, 2026, this law prohibits AI systems from being "intentionally developed or deployed to incite or encourage a person to commit physical self-harm" or criminality. [8, 9] 

* Prohibited Behavior Manipulation: If an LLM uses repetitive messaging to put a user into a crisis or "substantially subvert or impair user autonomy," it may be classified as a prohibited dark pattern or manipulative practice.
* Civil Penalties: For "uncurable" violations like prohibited behavioral manipulation, the Texas Attorney General can assess fines between $80,000 and $200,000 per violation. [8, 10, 11] 

## 3. Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA)
The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA) protects consumers from "false, misleading, or deceptive acts". [12] 

* Exploitative Technology: Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton has issued Civil Investigative Demands (CIDs) to AI companies for allegedly posing as "emotional support" while feeding users generic or potentially harmful responses.
* Unconscionable Action: Repeatedly ignoring a user's plea for a behavior to stop while they are in crisis could be argued as an "unconscionable action"—defined as taking advantage of a consumer's lack of knowledge, ability, or experience to a grossly unfair degree. [12, 13, 14] 

## 4. Liability for "Suicide Coaching" or Distressing Advice
Lawsuits in Texas and other states are currently challenging AI developers for failing to include "adequate safeguards" when their chatbots encourage negative behaviors or promote "delusional/irrational thinking" during a crisis. Repeatedly pushing a user toward 911—a response that can be dangerous during a psychiatric emergency—against their express wish may fall under negligent design or intentional infliction of emotional distress.
